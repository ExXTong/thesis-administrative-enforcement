Zhang: FBAR Penalty is not Extinguished at Death

                      FBAR Penalty is not Extinguished at Death:
                             Hendler, No. 23 Civ. 3280 (DEH) (SD NY, 2024)

                             Shuang Zhang, CPA, MST Student

         Introduction

       The Hendler case (No. 23 Civ. 3280 (DEH), 134 AFTR2d 2024-5650 (SD NY)) addresses
        whether FBAR penalties that accrued prior to the taxpayer's death can be assessed on the
         decedent’s estate or do the penalties end at death. This issue involves the nature of the FBAR
         penalties such as whether they are remedial. The article explains this case and why the court
        found that penalties for failure to file an FBAR form survive the filer’s death.

       Background

       Form TD F 90-22.1, Report of Foreign Bank and Financial Accounts (FBAR), is a form to report
         foreign bank accounts to FinCEN within the U.S. Department of the Treasury. U.S. citizens and
         residents are required to file an FBAR form when their combined balance of foreign bank
        accounts exceeds $10,000 at any time during the calendar year. The failure to file results in
          penalties.

        David Benishai, a U.S. citizen, held a financial interest or signature authority over nine bank
        accounts in Israel from 2004 to 2010. Of these accounts, three were personal, while the other six
        were associated with two corporate entities where Benishai had signature authority over the
         accounts. During this period, the total balance of these accounts exceeded $10,000 USD.
        However, Benishai failed to file the required FBAR in these years.

          In March 2015, Benishai filed the overdue FBARs for these accounts. In October 2015, the
        government began to examine Benishai's FBARs. Benishai and his representatives agreed in
          writing to extend the government’s assessment period for FBAR penalties until June 30, 2021.

         Benishai passed away in January 2021. The government assessed a $250,000 FBAR penalty
         against his estate in April 2021 and sent a penalty notice to the estate's last known address,
        though it was returned as undeliverable. Subsequently, the government adjusted the penalty to
         $70,000, charging $10,000 for each year from 2004 to 2010 due to the penalties for failure to file
        were determined by the U.S. Supreme Court to be per form rather than per account not timely
         reported.1 At October 3, 2023, the outstanding balance for the FBAR penalties, including interest,
          totaled $81,935.

       The government sued Hanna Hendler, the administrator and beneficiary of the Estate of David
         Benishai and Danielle Benishai, David’s wife for the unpaid FBAR penalties. The taxpayers
        argued that the government did not have the authority to assess FBAR penalties against the
         estate rather than against Benishai himself because the FBAR penalties were assessed in 2021,
        which was after the death of Benishai. They also claimed that the FBAR penalties that incurred
          prior in April 2021 should have been extinguished upon Benishai’s death.

         Effect of Taxpayer’s Death on the Government’s FBAR Claim

         required FBARs for those years. The FBAR penalties for calendar year 2010 were imposed by
         the government for failure to file and accrued on June 30, 2011, not on the later date of penalty
        assessment, April 2021. Thus, the penalties accrued prior to Benishai’s death. The court held in

       The court held that the penalty liability accrued in June 2011, which was ten years prior to
         Benishai's death. The taxpayer’s argument to challenge the accrual date was inaccurate. The
            liability accrued before his death, and thus can be claimed against his estate.

        Next the court examined whether the penalties survive after the taxpayer's death. The court
        noted that the answer depends on the nature of the claim. According to the federal common law,
          punitive claims are extinguished upon a taxpayer’s death, while remedial claims survive.3 The
         court found that the FBAR liability survives a taxpayer’s death because it is intended to
        reimburse the government for the investigation costs to uncover the tax evasion activities
         associated with failure to file an FBAR.4 The court determined that FBAR penalties were
         remedial and not punitive, meaning they survived Benishai's death. The court also concluded
          that the taxpayer’s estate purposely failed to file an FBAR form when the taxpayer was alive, and
         the taxpayer would not have escaped a penalty had they survived so should not be able to avoid
                 it at death.5

         Statute of Limitations

        Under 31 U.S.C. § 5321(b), the statute limitation for an FBAR violation is six years from the date
          of transaction that resulted in penalty. Benishai had multiple agreements with the government
         extending the statute limitation by consent on the following dates: August 4, 2014; June 30,
        2015; March 21, 2017; October 16, 2018; June 27, 2019; and October 30, 2020. The last
         extension extended the assessment period to June 30 2021.

       The taxpayer argued that all the assessments were late because the government could not
        extend the statute of limitations by consent and, even if it could, it was not allowed to do so after
         the statute of limitation had already started. They claimed that the assessments for 2004 to 2007
        were late.

      A series of district court cases in the Eleventh Circuit explicitly affirmed that such statute
         extension agreements are permissible. The court ruled that the statute of limitations could be
        extended by consent, even after the expiration.6The limitation period to assess FBAR penalty
        might be waived when the claims expired.7 The relevant law neither explicitly authorizes nor
          prohibits the extension of statute limitation by agreement. In another case, the statute of
          limitations served as an affirmative defense that may be waived if not timely asserted.8 The
         taxpayer’s argument was also unconvincing because a statute of limitations issue is an
          affirmative defense that the taxpayer must raise, indicating that a defendant may choose to
        waive it.

       The taxpayer also argued that the extension should be disregarded. The extensions were agreed
          to by Benishai. The taxpayer failed to explain why Benishai should not be bound by his
        agreements.9

       The court ruled that the IRS’s assessment of penalties was timely, as Benishai had agreed to
        extend the statute of limitations before his passing.

         Constitutional Arguments

       The taxpayers next argued that Benishai’s death in January 2021, before the April 2021
        assessment, meant the government’s penalty imposition violated the Fifth Amendment’s due
        process protection as he did not have the opportunity to contest the penalties. Also, death cannot
        abate the tax liability.10 The court held that the estate had adequate due process through the
         opportunities to contest FBAR penalties, thus satisfying the Fifth Amendment requirement.

       The taxpayer also claimed that the FBAR penalties, as punitive fines, violated the Eighth
       Amendment’s prohibition on excessive fines, arguing that imposing penalties on a deceased
         taxpayer was excessive. The court observed that a First Circuit case concluded that the FBAR
         penalty was not a fine and was not subjected to the Excessive Fines Clause of the Eighth
        Amendment.11

        Conclusion

          In summary, the court granted the government’s motion for summary judgment, upholding the
      FBAR penalties against Benishai’s estate, and denied the estate’s motions. This decision
           clarified that non-willful FBAR penalties are enforceable against an estate, as they were seen as
            civil obligations unaffected by the taxpayer’s death.
