PUNISHING CORRUPTION OFFENDERS AND ​
                RECOVERING STATE ASSETS

                              Iva Turisnur1, Hulaimi2, Duwi Handoko3, ​
                                Beni Sukri4, Mulyani Rody Muin5

                                             Abstract
 The eradication of corruption is not solely oriented towards the criminal punishment of offenders, but also
 towards the recovery of state financial losses. Such recovery may be pursued through various legal instruments,
 including the payment of compensation equivalent to state losses, the seizure and confiscation of assets, civil
 proceedings in certain circumstances, the tracing of proceeds of money laundering offences, and international
 legal cooperation. This study examines two issues: first, the legal framework governing the recovery of state
 financial losses resulting from corruption offences; and second, the legal obstacles to its implementation and the
 prospects for strengthening the applicable legal framework. This study employs normative legal research using
 statutory and conceptual approaches, conducted through a literature review of relevant legal materials. The
 analysis indicates that the legal framework for recovering state financial losses provides several complementary
 instruments. However, their implementation requires clarity regarding the relationship between the various
 mechanisms,  evidentiary  accuracy,  protection  of  third-party  rights, and  effective enforcement  of court
 judgments. A comparison with the United States and South Korea indicates that both countries have more
 comprehensive legal frameworks for asset recovery, whereas Indonesia continues to face significant challenges,
 particularly in cross-border asset recovery. Asset recovery must also be assessed carefully by distinguishing
 between the amount of compensation ordered by the court, assets identified or seized, assets confiscated, and the
 value actually recovered by the state. Accordingly, optimising the recovery of state financial losses requires
 regulatory harmonisation, procedural clarity, stronger inter-agency coordination, and effective monitoring of the
 enforcement of court judgments, while safeguarding the principle of legality and the right to a fair trial.

 Keywords: Corruption offences; state financial losses; asset recovery; compensation for state losses; asset
 confiscation.

INTRODUCTION
      Corruption offences constitute not only violations of criminal law norms but also give rise to
 serious concerns regarding the protection of state finances, the administration of government, and the
 fulfilment of the public interest. The harm caused by corruption extends beyond the depletion of public
 assets or resources; it also entails the loss of opportunities for the state to utilise those resources for
 public services and development. Accordingly, the success of anti-corruption efforts should not be
 measured solely by the number of cases successfully prosecuted or the severity of the criminal
 penalties imposed on offenders, but also by the state's ability to recover assets and funds associated
 with such offences. From this perspective, punishing offenders and recovering state funds are two
 interrelated objectives, although they require distinct legal instruments and measures of success.
      Indonesia's legal framework for combating corruption provides instruments that link criminal
 liability to the recovery of state finances. Law Number 31 of 1999 on the Eradication of Corruption, as
amended by Law Number 20 of 2001, inter alia, provides for an additional criminal penalty in the form
 of payment of compensation equivalent to state losses, as well as the confiscation of certain property
 under Article 18. Meanwhile, Article 4 stipulates that the recovery of state financial losses or losses to

     Although legal instruments are available, the existence of a normative legal basis does not, in
itself, guarantee that assets associated with criminal offences can be identified, secured through lawful
procedures, confiscated in accordance with applicable legal requirements, and optimally recovered by
the state. Difficulties may arise when assets are transferred to third parties, concealed through financial
transactions, held abroad, or no longer remain under the convicted person's control when a court
judgment is to be enforced. These circumstances demonstrate that asset recovery requires a series of
interconnected measures, ranging from asset identification and tracing, lawful preservation through
appropriate legal measures, and the establishment of the connection between the assets and the
criminal offence, to the enforcement of court judgments and the actual return of assets to the state.
     Research by Komalasari and Mustafa identifies institutional barriers, weak institutions, and low
levels of public trust in public officials as factors that impede anti-corruption efforts and asset recovery
in  Indonesia. A  holistic approach  integrating  infrastructure development,  social  rehabilitation,
community empowerment, and regulatory reform, together with the incorporation of civil asset
forfeiture, is considered an important component of an effective anti-corruption strategy. Accordingly,
asset recovery cannot be achieved solely through the imposition of criminal sanctions; rather, it also
requires the restoration of public trust in public office as a prerequisite for its success.1
     These issues may become increasingly complex when asset recovery instruments must be
implemented while safeguarding legal certainty, property rights, and the protection of bona fide third
parties.  Seizure  during  the  investigation  stage,  for example, must be  distinguished from  the
confiscation of assets as a legal consequence imposed in accordance with the applicable procedures
and legal authority. Similarly, the amount of compensation ordered by a court cannot automatically be
equated with the amount of money actually recovered. The state may have obtained a judgment
ordering  the payment of compensation equivalent  to  state losses, yet the actual recovery and
enforcement of that judgment remain dependent on the circumstances surrounding the assets and the
implementation of the available legal mechanisms.

is insufficiently responsive to the characteristics of complex, transnational, and difficult-to-prove
white-collar crime, as asset forfeiture can only be pursued through a criminal conviction, thereby
allowing   offenders   opportunities   to  conceal   or   transfer  the  proceeds  of  crime.  The
non-conviction-based asset forfeiture (NCB) paradigm is considered more effective because it focuses
on in rem proceedings, the balance of probabilities standard of proof, and the reversal of the burden of
proof onto asset owners, while continuing to protect the rights of bona fide third parties. Their analysis

      Fauzi, Anditya, and Fitriana present restorative justice as an approach in criminology and

victims’ interests, strengthen social security, and prevent recidivism. In corruption cases, restorative
justice is pursued through asset recovery mechanisms, enabling the state to recover its financial losses
and the public to obtain a measure of justice. Their study offers one perspective for examining the
relationship between the recovery of losses and the objectives of law enforcement. In the context of
Indonesian law, however, any such approach must be assessed in light of the applicable legislation, the
specific nature of corruption offences, and the principle that the recovery of state financial losses does
not automatically constitute grounds for exemption from criminal punishment.3 The need to integrate
legal instruments is also evident in cases involving cross-border assets. Recovering assets located
outside Indonesia’s jurisdiction requires not only the capacity to trace such assets, but also cooperation
with the state in which the assets are located, compliance with the legal requirements of the requested
state, and coordination among relevant institutions.
      Arifin, Utari, and Subondo explain that the recovery of proceeds of corruption located abroad
may be pursued through formal channels involving mutual legal assistance (MLA) and informal
channels  through  diplomatic  relations. The  process  consists  of  several  stages, namely  asset
identification and tracing, legal proceedings, and asset confiscation. The obstacles encountered include
differences between legal systems, inadequate judicial decisions, the government's political will, and
the application of banking secrecy laws in certain countries. Accordingly, bilateral agreements,
enhanced law enforcement capacity, and strengthened legal frameworks and supporting infrastructure
are necessary. The roles of the Attorney General's Office, the Indonesian National Central Bureau of
Interpol (NCB Interpol Indonesia), the Corruption Eradication Commission (KPK), the Ministry of
Law and Human Rights, and the Ministry of Foreign Affairs complement one another in asset recovery
efforts in accordance with applicable law. The success of asset recovery therefore depends on
cooperation among institutions and law enforcement authorities, both domestically and internationally.
Although this study provides a useful historical foundation, subsequent regulatory developments and
practices in international cooperation must be examined to determine its relevance to the current legal
framework in Indonesia.4

      In light of these issues, research on the recovery of state financial losses must go beyond
separately  cataloguing  the  legal  provisions  governing  compensation  payments,  seizure,  asset
confiscation, and  civil proceedings. The central issue  is how these instruments interact within
Indonesia's legal system and how normative obstacles and challenges in law enforcement affect the
actual recovery of state financial losses. Unlike previous studies that primarily examine particular asset
recovery mechanisms, restorative justice approaches, or the recovery of assets located abroad, this
study analyses  the  relationship between  the  legal framework governing the available recovery
instruments,  the  legal and procedural obstacles to  their implementation, and the directions for
strengthening the law to optimise the actual recovery of state financial losses. This integrated analysis
constitutes the specific research gap addressed by this study, without assuming that previous research
has not examined related issues.
    On this basis, the study addresses two research questions. First, how are the legal instruments for
recovering state financial losses resulting from corruption offences regulated under Indonesian law?
Second, what legal obstacles arise in the implementation of asset recovery instruments, and how can
the legal framework be strengthened to support the optimal recovery of state financial losses? This
study aims to analyse the legal framework governing the instruments for recovering state financial
losses and to identify legal obstacles to their implementation, thereby formulating directions for legal
reform that preserve the effectiveness of asset recovery, legal certainty, and the protection of the rights
of interested parties.

METHOD
     This study employs normative legal research using statutory and conceptual approaches. The
statutory approach is used to examine the legal provisions governing instruments for recovering state
financial losses, including compensation payments, the seizure and confiscation of assets, civil claims,
anti-money laundering measures, and cross-border legal cooperation. The conceptual approach is used
to  distinguish between the concepts of  state financial losses, proceeds of crime, compensation
equivalent to state losses, seizure, confiscation, and the actual realisation of asset recovery.
     Legal materials were collected through a literature review of relevant legislation and legal
literature. The materials were analysed qualitatively by examining the legal basis, requirements,
functions, and interrelationships of the various instruments, while identifying issues concerning legal
certainty and regulatory consistency. This study is not intended to empirically measure the success rate
of asset recovery. Accordingly, conclusions concerning the effectiveness of asset recovery practices are
limited to analyses of legal materials and literature that can be substantiated.

    confiscation or payment pursuant to a court judgment, and the actual return of assets to the state.
   This process must not be equated with the imposition of criminal punishment on offenders.

        Under Indonesian law, an analysis of recovery instruments must distinguish at least four
   concepts: seizure, namely the securing of assets for the purposes of legal proceedings; confiscation,
  namely the taking of assets pursuant to applicable legal authority and procedures; compensation
   equivalent to state losses (uang pengganti), namely an additional criminal penalty imposed on a
  convicted person in accordance with statutory provisions; and actual recovery, namely the value of
   assets or payments successfully returned to the state. This distinction is essential to ensure that the
  amount of state financial losses, the amount of compensation ordered by a court, the value of
   seized assets, and the amount actually returned to the state treasury are not treated as equivalent
   figures.
   1.​ Legal Basis for Compensation Payments and Asset Confiscation
        The principal legal basis for recovering state finances through corruption proceedings is
  Law Number 31 of 1999 on the Eradication of Corruption, as amended by Law Number 20 of
  2001. Article 18(1) provides for additional criminal penalties, including the confiscation of
  movable or immovable  property, whether  tangible or intangible, used in or obtained from
   corruption offences, including property substituted for such assets; payment of compensation
   equivalent to state losses, capped at the value of the assets obtained from corruption offences;
   closure of a company for a specified period; and revocation of certain rights or benefits previously
  granted by the government to the convicted person. Accordingly, compensation payments and
   property confiscation constitute distinct legal instruments, although both may contribute to the
  recovery of the proceeds of corruption.
        The  limitation on compensation payments  set out in Article 18(1)(b)  is particularly
   significant. This provision links the amount payable to the assets obtained from corruption
   offences. Consequently, compensation equivalent to state losses should not be understood as a fine
  whose amount may be determined independently of the proceeds of the offence. An analysis of
   court judgments must consider the basis for determining the amount imposed, the connection
  between the relevant assets and the criminal offence, and the manner in which the obligation is
  formulated in the operative part of the judgment. At the same time, the amount of state financial
   losses and the value of assets obtained by an offender do not necessarily constitute identical
   categories. Both must be established and analysed in accordance with the relevant elements and
   legal provisions applicable to the case concerned.
          Article 18(2) further provides that, if a convicted person fails to pay the compensation
  within one month after the judgment has become legally binding, the person's assets may be seized
  by the public prosecutor and auctioned to satisfy the payment obligation. Article 18(3) prescribes
   criminal  consequences  where  the  convicted  person's  assets  are  insufficient  to  cover  the
  compensation, subject to the sentence specified in the judgment and the limits established by law.
  These provisions demonstrate that a judgment ordering compensation requires a subsequent
  enforcement stage.

    2.​ Civil Claims as an Asset Recovery Instrument
           In addition to criminal law instruments, the Corruption Eradication Law provides for civil
   claims in certain circumstances. Article 32 provides for the referral of a case to the State Attorney
    or the injured government agency to initiate a civil claim where investigators determine that one or
   more elements of a corruption offence cannot be sufficiently established by evidence, although
    actual state financial losses have occurred. The same article also stipulates that an acquittal in
    corruption proceedings does not extinguish the right to seek recovery of state financial losses
   through civil proceedings. Articles 33 and 34 provide for the possibility of bringing civil claims
    against heirs where a suspect dies during the investigation stage or a defendant dies during trial
    proceedings, provided that actual state financial losses have occurred.
         These provisions demonstrate that the recovery of state finances does not depend entirely
   on  the  successful  imposition of  criminal punishment.  Civil proceedings may serve  as an
   independent recovery instrument in circumstances specifically prescribed by law. However, the
    availability of this avenue does not authorise the state to take a person's property solely on the basis
    of suspicion or a finding of financial loss. A civil claim still requires a legal basis, proof of the
    losses, identification of the party responsible, and compliance with applicable civil procedure.
   Meanwhile, proposals for legal reform have also introduced other non-criminal instruments, such
    as a Deferred Prosecution Agreement (DPA), which may be applied to corporations subject to
   requirements concerning  the recovery of  state losses and other qualifying conditions.7 The
    provisions governing claims against heirs must not be interpreted as transferring criminal liability
    to  heirs;  rather,  their purpose concerns claims  for  financial recovery under the conditions
    established by law.

          Conceptually, civil claims and the additional criminal penalty of compensation equivalent
   to state losses serve different functions. Compensation is imposed within the criminal sentencing
  framework and is governed by the specific provisions of the Corruption Eradication Law. Civil
   claims, by contrast, provide a mechanism for seeking recovery on the basis of applicable civil law
  and its requirements. The choice of mechanism must take into account the circumstances of the
   case, the status of the criminal proceedings, the sufficiency of the evidence, the parties who may be
  held legally responsible, and the type of loss to be recovered. Selecting the appropriate instrument
   is also necessary to prevent overlapping claims or inconsistent calculations of recovered amounts.

   3.​ Asset Tracing and the Anti-Money Laundering Approach
         Asset recovery requires the capacity to identify and trace the proceeds of crime, including
  where assets have been moved, transferred, spent, or concealed through financial transactions. In
   this regard, Law Number 8 of 2010 on the Prevention and Eradication of Money Laundering
  provides a relevant legal framework because it includes corruption among the predicate offences.
   Articles 3 and 4  regulate conduct involving  the  transfer or conversion of  assets and the
  concealment or disguising of their origin or ownership, provided that the elements of a money
  laundering offence are satisfied. Accordingly, the anti-money laundering approach may assist law
  enforcement authorities in addressing financial flows and assets associated with corruption. Its
   application, however, must remain grounded in the relevant statutory elements and evidentiary
   requirements.
        The  relationship  between  corruption  proceedings  and money  laundering  must  be
  understood accurately. Not every asset owned by a suspect or convicted person automatically
   constitutes the proceeds of crime, and not every unusual transaction, in itself, establishes money
   laundering.  Asset  tracing must be  directed  towards  establishing an  evidentially  verifiable
  connection between the assets, transaction flows, the parties exercising control over or enjoying the
   benefits of those assets, and the criminal offence underlying the proceedings. This framework is
  important for distinguishing preliminary preservation measures from a final determination of the
   legal status of the assets. Any determination and confiscation of assets must remain subject to
  lawful procedures, including the protection of third parties who can establish a legitimate interest
   in the assets concerned.
          In practice, corruption investigations and money laundering investigations may perform
  complementary functions: the former investigate corruption offences and the criminal liability of
   the offenders, while the latter may be used to investigate dealings in the proceeds of crime where
   the relevant elements are satisfied. Inter-agency coordination, access to financial information
  within the limits of legal authority, and documentation of asset flows are important to ensure that
   tracing results can be used in legal proceedings. However, the technical capacity to trace assets
  must not be equated with the legal authority to confiscate them. Financial intelligence, for
  example, may provide leads for an investigation, but coercive measures and confiscation must have
  a legal basis and satisfy the applicable evidentiary and procedural requirements.

          Indonesia ratified the United Nations Convention against Corruption (UNCAC) through
   Law Number 7 of 2006. UNCAC recognises asset recovery as an important component of
    international cooperation against corruption. However, ratification of the Convention does not
    confer direct authority on Indonesian officials to seize assets in another country. Implementation
   remains dependent on legal cooperation mechanisms, applicable requirements, and action by the
    authorities of the state in which the assets are located. International cooperation must therefore be
   understood as a means of requesting and providing legal assistance, rather than as a substitute for
   domestic procedures or the legal sovereignty of the requested state.
          Cross-border challenges also demonstrate the importance of initiating asset tracing at an
    early stage of a case. If ownership identification, the collection of transaction records, and requests
    for legal assistance are undertaken only after criminal proceedings have been ongoing for an
   extended period, the assets may already have been transferred or their ownership structures may
   have become increasingly complex. From a legal and regulatory perspective, the effectiveness of
    international cooperation therefore cannot be assessed solely by the existence of treaties or
   normative instruments. It is also necessary to consider the speed of coordination, the quality of
   submitted documentation, the compatibility of requests with the law of the requested state, and the
    capacity to manage assets that have been secured until the recovery process is complete.
    5.​ The Status of the Asset Forfeiture Bill and the Limits of Existing Law
        Any discussion of strengthening asset recovery must distinguish between legal instruments
    already in force and proposed reforms that remain within the legislative process. On 18 August
   2026, Commission III of the Indonesian House of Representatives (DPR RI) announced plans to
   continue deliberations on the bill by holding additional public hearings to gather public input.
   Subsequently, on 25 August 2026, the DPR reiterated that further examination was required
    regarding several issues, including non-conviction-based asset forfeiture, the protection of third
    parties who lawfully own assets, the reversal of the burden of proof, and the governance of
    confiscated assets. These developments indicate that the Asset Forfeiture Bill remains a draft
   whose substantive provisions are under consideration and cannot yet be treated as an enacted law
    in force.
          This distinction is important because non-conviction-based asset forfeiture (NCB) is one of
    the models being considered in efforts to reform the law. It must be distinguished from asset
    confiscation imposed as an additional criminal penalty through criminal proceedings. In reporting
   published by the DPR in August 2026, the mechanism for confiscating assets without a criminal
    conviction was identified as one of the issues requiring further examination. Concerns have also

   circumstances prescribed by law. Meanwhile, the Law on the Prevention and Eradication of Money
   Laundering provides legal mechanisms for dealing with assets associated with money laundering
    offences.
         At the preliminary stage of a case, the recovery of state losses may even extinguish the
    criminal offence if it occurs before the investigation stage, on the reasoning that the element of
    state financial loss, which constitutes one of the elements of corruption, has ceased to exist.9
   However, the criminal law policy governing the recovery of state financial losses arising from
    corruption  has  been  considered  insufficiently  effective,  creating  a  need  for  regulatory
    simplification and specific legal provisions from the earliest stages of case handling.10 Limitations
    in criminal procedural law have also been considered an obstacle to maximising the recovery of
    state assets, indicating the need for alternative solutions.11 Accordingly, the legal problem does not
    lie solely in the absence of asset recovery instruments, but also in the effectiveness of existing
    provisions and the need to address regulatory limitations and gaps through lawful legal reform.
         Based on the foregoing, the recovery of state financial losses under Indonesian law does not
    rely on a single instrument. The existing framework combines criminal law instruments, including
   compensation payments and property confiscation; civil claims in circumstances prescribed by
   law; and instruments relating to the tracing and handling of proceeds of money laundering
    offences.  For  cross-border  assets,  these  mechanisms  must  be  supported by  international
   cooperation consistent with the laws and procedures of the relevant states.
          Thus, Indonesian law provides several legal bases for recovering assets associated with
    corruption, but the existence of these instruments does not, in itself, guarantee optimal recovery.
   This mapping demonstrates that the recovery of state financial losses in corruption cases involves
    instruments with different legal bases, functions, and implementation mechanisms. Compensation
   payments, seizure, confiscation, civil claims, anti-money laundering measures, and cross-border
   cooperation cannot be treated as mechanisms that simply substitute for one another. The analysis

B.​ Legal Obstacles and Legal Reform to Optimise the Recovery of State Financial Losses
         The regulation of various asset recovery instruments does not, in itself, guarantee that state
    financial losses will be recovered in full. Legal problems arise when asset tracing, proof of the
    relationship between assets and criminal offences, the imposition of additional criminal penalties,
   and the enforcement of court judgments do not operate effectively as an integrated process. Legal
    obstacles must be analysed by  distinguishing between  substantive  legal  issues, procedural
    difficulties in applying the available instruments, and the enforcement of final and binding court
   judgments.
          This analysis must also take account of developments in Indonesia’s national criminal law.
   The entry into force of the national Criminal Code and the Criminal Penalties Adjustment Law
    requires a reassessment of the relationship between general criminal law provisions and special
    criminal law provisions. Legal reform must be based on a comprehensive examination of the
    applicable legislation, rather than relying solely on provisions that have traditionally served as
    references in corruption eradication efforts.
    1.​ Legal Certainty and Regulatory Harmonisation
        One of the fundamental issues in recovering state financial losses is the need for legal
    certainty regarding the legal basis, requirements, and legal consequences of each instrument.
   Compensation for state losses, seizure, asset confiscation, civil claims, and the handling of money
    laundering offences each have distinct characteristics and procedures. If the relationship between
    these instruments is not properly understood, their application may create uncertainty regarding the
    stages to be followed, the legal status of assets under consideration, and the mechanisms available
    to achieve actual recovery.
           In the literature, provisions governing the recovery of state financial losses resulting from
    corruption offences are classified into criminal law norms and civil law norms.12 Criminal law
   norms are implemented through asset confiscation and compensation payments, whereas civil law
   norms operate through claims against convicted persons or their heirs. This classification clarifies
    the types of instruments available, but does not, in  itself, explain the relationship between
    procedural stages, the status of assets, and the mechanisms for implementing recovery. The issue
   becomes more complex because Article 4 of the Corruption Eradication Law (UU Tipikor), which
   provides that the recovery of state financial losses does not extinguish criminal liability, has been
   considered in need of reconstruction to accommodate restorative justice principles and legal
    incentives for offenders who cooperate with the authorities.13

   between relevant provisions also requires a clear connecting principle. The principle of systematic
    lex specialis, for example, may serve as a reference for judges to ensure that judgments remain

    transaction flows, and establish the legal relationship between the assets and the criminal offence.

    person's name, or converted into forms that are difficult to trace.16 In practice, investigators and
    public prosecutors often encounter  difficulties in seizing assets because offenders attempt to
   conceal them through money laundering or transfers of ownership to other persons. However,
    difficulty in locating assets does not necessarily indicate a legal vacuum. For example, in practice

    applicable legal requirements. Financial intelligence, transaction records, or indications of a
    relationship between assets and an offender may provide leads for tracing activities, but they do
    not, by themselves, replace the evidentiary requirements applicable to seizure or confiscation.

    5.​ Directions for Legal Reform to Optimise Asset Recovery
             First, regulatory harmonisation must ensure consistency between general criminal law
    provisions, corruption offences legislation, anti-money laundering legislation, criminal procedural
    law, and the rules governing the enforcement of criminal penalties. This process must be based on
   a comprehensive examination of the legislation in force, including amendments and transitional
    provisions, to ensure that legal reform proposals do not rely on provisions that have been repealed,
   amended, or adjusted. Harmonisation should also clarify the relationship between the various asset
   recovery instruments, the legal requirements governing  their application, and the safeguards
    available to third parties whose rights may be affected.
          Second, asset tracing and preservation mechanisms must be supported by clear procedures
   governing legal authority, information exchange, record-keeping, oversight, and the protection of
    third-party rights. Clear procedures are necessary to ensure that action against assets can be taken
    in a timely manner while remaining subject to legal scrutiny. Reform should not be limited to
   expanding the powers of law enforcement authorities. It must also ensure that those powers are
    exercised for legitimate purposes, through proportionate procedures, and under effective oversight.
   Coordination and lawful information exchange between relevant institutions should strengthen
    continuity from asset identification and investigation through prosecution and the securing of
    property.
           Third, the enforcement of compensation orders should be more systematically integrated
   with asset information management and the execution of court judgments. The legal framework
   governing compensation payments should  clarify the procedures for payment and the legal
   consequences of non-compliance, thereby reducing the risk of inconsistent interpretation during
   enforcement. The assessment of asset recovery performance should not be based solely on the
   amount of compensation ordered by the court. It should distinguish between the amount ordered,
    the amount paid, the value of assets successfully realised through enforcement, and the net amount
    actually received by the state. These indicators would provide a more accurate basis for evaluating
    the practical effectiveness of recovery measures.

    institution's legal authority, lawful data exchange, and administrative continuity throughout the
   recovery process. This includes coordination between asset tracing, investigation, prosecution, the
   management of seized property, judgment enforcement, and the recording of state revenue. In
    cross-border cases, cooperation through Mutual Legal Assistance must be strengthened by ensuring
   compliance with  the  applicable  international instruments and the legal requirements of the
    requested state. Secured assets must also be managed appropriately throughout the process until
    their return or transfer to the state has been completed.

CONCLUSION
         The recovery of state financial losses resulting from corruption offences in Indonesia is
   supported by  several  legal  instruments,  each  with  a  distinct  legal  basis,  function, and
   implementation mechanism. These instruments include compensation payments, the seizure and
    confiscation of assets, civil claims in circumstances prescribed by law, the handling of money
    laundering offences, and cross-border legal cooperation. The application of each instrument must
    take account of the legal basis for the action, the applicable legal requirements, evidentiary
    procedures, and the protection of third-party rights. The existence of legal instruments does not, in
     itself, guarantee the recovery of all state financial losses. The amount ordered by a court, assets
    identified or seized, and the amount actually returned to the state are distinct matters and must be
    assessed separately. A comparison with the United States and South Korea indicates that both
    countries have more comprehensive asset recovery frameworks, while Indonesia continues to face
    significant challenges, particularly in cross-border cooperation.
