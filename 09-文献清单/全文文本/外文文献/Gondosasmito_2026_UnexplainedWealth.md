                                Abstrak
Pemulihan aset hasil korupsi di Indonesia masih menghadapi keterbatasan karena
mekanisme perampasan pada umumnya bergantung pada  proses pidana dan
keterkaitan  aset dengan  tindak  pidana yang  dapat  dibuktikan.  Penelitian  ini
menganalisis legitimasi doktrin unexplained wealth, merumuskan desain pembuktian
terbalik yang proporsional, dan menguji kebutuhan pengawasan yang lebih kuat
terhadap kekayaan aparat penegak hukum. Penelitian menggunakan metode hukum
normatif  dengan  pendekatan  perundang-undangan,  konseptual,  kasus,  dan
perbandingan hukum.  Hasil  penelitian menunjukkan bahwa pembalikan beban
pembuktian dapat dirancang tanpa meniadakan praduga tidak bersalah apabila
ditempatkan dalam mekanisme in rem dan didahului kewajiban negara menunjukkan
ketidakwajaran kekayaan secara objektif. Penelitian menawarkan model pembuktian
terbalik berimbang bertingkat yang terdiri atas pemicu objektif, pembuktian prima
facie oleh negara, kesempatan pemilik menjelaskan asal-usul harta berdasarkan
keseimbangan kemungkinan, dan putusan pengadilan dengan jaminan perlindungan
hak. Terhadap aparat penegak hukum, model tersebut perlu dilengkapi pemeriksaan
independen untuk mengurangi konflik kepentingan. Formulasi ini dapat menjadi salah
satu rujukan dalam pembaruan hukum perampasan aset di Indonesia.
Kata Kunci: unexplained wealth; perampasan aset; pembuktian terbalik; aparat
penegak hukum; non-conviction based forfeiture.
                                   Abstract
Asset recovery in Indonesian corruption cases remains constrained because confiscation
generally depends on criminal proceedings and proof linking property to a predicate
offence. This study examines the legal legitimacy of the unexplained wealth doctrine,
formulates a proportionate reversed-burden model, and assesses the need for stronger
scrutiny of assets held by law-enforcement officers. It employs normative legal research
using statutory, conceptual, case, and comparative approaches. The study finds that
shifting the evidentiary burden can be designed consistently with the presumption of
innocence when  it operates within an in rem mechanism and only after the State
establishes objective grounds showing a material disparity between assets and lawful
income. The article proposes a tiered balanced reversed-burden model consisting of an
objective trigger, a prima facie burden on the State, an opportunity for the asset holder to
establish lawful origin on the balance of probabilities, and a judicial determination
accompanied by procedural safeguards. For law-enforcement officers, the mechanism

INTRODUCTION
     Corruption in Indonesia is not only a question of punishing offenders; it is also a
question of recovering property that has not returned to the  State. Indonesia
Corruption Watch (ICW), in its monitoring of corruption judgments for 2024, recorded
state financial losses of approximately IDR 330.9 trillion, while the recovery rate was
only 4.84 percent, consisting of IDR 316 billion in fines and IDR 16.58 trillion in
replacement money. Of the 1,768 judgments monitored, the average prison sentence
was only three years and three months.1
     Existing confiscation instruments have likewise not been used optimally. Only
63.56 percent of defendants were ordered to pay replacement money under Article 18
of the Anti-Corruption Law, while money-laundering provisions were applied to only
25 defendants. At the investigation stage, of 364 cases, only 48 used Article 18 and only
five used anti-money-laundering instruments.2
    The difficulty becomes particularly acute when the person concerned is a law-
enforcement officer. The Zarof Ricar case, involving a former official of the Supreme
Court, is a prominent illustration. The court considered that no lawful income could
plausibly explain cash equivalent to approximately IDR 915 billion and 51 kilograms of
gold held by a civil servant, and the defendant failed to demonstrate a lawful origin for
those assets. His declared lawful wealth in the 2023 tax return was approximately IDR
8.82 billion, creating a disparity of more than one hundred times the declared figure.3
    The case demonstrates two points. First, the logic of unexplained wealth has
already been used factually by Indonesian judges when assessing assets that cannot
reasonably be reconciled with lawful income. Second, that logic still operates only
because there is an underlying criminal case. Without a predicate proceeding, assets of
a comparable scale may have no clear, autonomous confiscation pathway under
existing law.
    As of September 2026, the Asset Forfeiture Bill remained under deliberation in
Commission III of the House of Representatives, and its substantive design had not yet
been finalised. Parliamentary leaders stated a target of completing deliberations by 15
December 2026. This fluid legislative position reinforces the relevance of the present
study because the architecture of unexplained-wealth confiscation, standards of proof,
and procedural safeguards remain open to legislative design.4

    The central research questions are therefore threefold: how the unexplained
wealth doctrine and a reversed evidentiary burden can be justified within the
Indonesian legal system in light of the presumption of innocence and property rights;
how a fair reversed-burden model should be formulated for assets whose lawful origin
cannot be explained; and why, and by what mechanism, the doctrine should be applied
with strengthened scrutiny to law-enforcement officers.
     This study seeks  to formulate norms as  ius constituendum  for  possible
incorporation into future asset-forfeiture legislation. Its principal contribution is a
tiered balanced reversed-burden model combined with a specific clause for law-
enforcement officers. Earlier Indonesian scholarship has discussed the balance-of-
probabilities approach and non-conviction-based asset forfeiture, while international
literature has examined illicit enrichment and unexplained wealth more generally. The
gap addressed here lies in integrating a staged evidentiary structure with a heightened,
independently administered framework for law-enforcement officers.5

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Legitimacy of the Unexplained Wealth Doctrine and the Reversed Burden of
Proof
    A reversed burden concerning the lawful origin of assets can be designed
consistently with constitutional guarantees and international human-rights law,
provided that the mechanism is formulated as restorative in rem confiscation rather
than as a substitute form of criminal punishment.
     Unexplained wealth refers to property whose value is materially inconsistent with
the owner’s lawful income and whose origin cannot be reasonably explained. The
doctrine may take two different legal forms. Illicit enrichment treats an unjustified
increase in wealth as an offence against a person (in personam), whereas an
unexplained wealth order or comparable  civil-recovery mechanism  treats  the
unexplained asset itself as the object of an in rem proceeding. This article adopts the
second model because it is more compatible with the presumption of innocence when
accompanied by appropriate procedural safeguards.8
    The United Nations Convention against Corruption (UNCAC), ratified by Indonesia
through Law No. 7 of 2006, provides several normative entry points. Article 20 invites
States Parties to consider criminalising illicit enrichment of public officials; Article
31(8) allows consideration of a requirement that an offender demonstrate the lawful
origin of alleged proceeds of crime; and Article 54(1)(c) encourages measures for
confiscation without a criminal conviction in appropriate circumstances.
    FATF Recommendation 4 likewise encourages effective confiscation mechanisms,
including non-conviction-based confiscation and, where consistent with domestic law,
mechanisms requiring an offender to demonstrate the lawful origin of property liable
to confiscation. In Europe, Article 16 of Directive (EU) 2024/1260 requires Member
States to provide a form of unexplained-wealth confiscation, with implementation due
by 23 November 2026. Its minimum scope concerns property linked to criminal
organisations, while Member States may adopt broader rules.9
    The principal objection is that reversing the evidentiary burden may conflict with
Article 8(1) of Law No. 48 of 2009 on Judicial Power. That objection can be answered
through three distinctions.

       2.  Restorative character. In rem confiscation does  not, by  itself, impose

      Article 28H(4) of the 1945 Constitution protects property against arbitrary
deprivation, while Article 28J(2) permits statutory limitations for legitimate public
purposes.  Accordingly,  unexplained-wealth  confiscation can be  constitutionally
defensible where it is clearly regulated by statute, imposed by a court, proportionate to
the proven unexplained portion of the assets, and accompanied by a meaningful right
of defence. Constitutional Court Decision No. 77/PUU-XII/2014, concerning evidentiary
provisions in the Anti-Money Laundering Law, further demonstrates that a limited
reversed burden is not inherently incompatible with the Constitution.
     Indonesian positive law already recognises limited forms of reversed proof, but
they remain tied to specific offences or criminal proceedings.

    The Zarof Ricar case shows that the absence of a satisfactory explanation for the
origin of property has already been treated judicially as relevant to confiscation. Yet the
construction still depends on an underlying gratification prosecution. The regulatory
gap is therefore the absence of a self-standing unexplained-wealth mechanism.
A Tiered Balanced Reversed-Burden Model
    A fair model should operate through four sequential stages: an objective trigger,
a prima facie burden on the State, a conditional shift of the burden to the property
holder, and an in rem judicial determination supported by rights safeguards. The
burden should not  shift  until the State  first establishes objective grounds for
questioning the lawful origin of the property.

     Stage 2 - Prima facie burden on the State. The applicant must identify the property
and  its  beneficial  controller, including nominee or family arrangements where
supported by evidence, and demonstrate why its value is materially inconsistent with
lawful income. The proposed standard is reasonable grounds to suspect. The State need
not prove a predicate offence at this stage because the purpose of the proceeding is to
test the legal status and explainability of the property, not to convict the holder of a
crime.
     Stage 3 - Shifted burden. Once the State has met the prima facie threshold, the
property holder should have a genuine opportunity to explain lawful origin, for example
by inheritance, gift, business income, investment returns, or other legitimate sources.
The standard should be the balance of probabilities, lower than the criminal standard
of beyond reasonable doubt. Statements made solely to explain the property should not
automatically be used to prosecute the holder criminally, except in proceedings
concerning intentionally false statements, thereby preserving the privilege against self-
incrimination.
     Stage 4  - In rem determination and safeguards. The court should order
confiscation only of the portion of property that remains unexplained after the full
evidentiary process, not the entirety of the holder’s assets. Mandatory safeguards
should include access to counsel and case materials, protection of bona fide third
parties, appeal and cassation, State compensation where a confiscation order is later
shown to have been erroneous, and a clearly defined look-back period.

progressive  law, procedural innovation  is defensible only  insofar as  it serves
substantive justice while remaining controlled by legal safeguards.13

Strengthened Application to Law-Enforcement Officers and Comparative Lessons
     Law-enforcement  officers  should  be  subject  to  the  unexplained-wealth
mechanism through heightened scrutiny and independent examination, not because
their rights are diminished, but because the nature of their public powers and the
structural risk of conflicts of interest justify more rigorous verification.
Reasons for Special Treatment
       1.  Coercive authority. Police officers, prosecutors, judges, investigators, and
         other justice officials may arrest, detain, seize, prosecute, or adjudicate. Such
        powers support a correspondingly high fiduciary obligation to the public.
       2.  Low-visibility corruption. Bribery connected with case handling may not
        produce a readily measurable state-financial loss. ICW monitoring indicated
          that  state-loss  offences dominated corruption  cases, while  trading  in
          influence,  bribery,  or  case  brokerage may  leave  unexplained  asset
         accumulation as one of the most visible financial traces.14
       3.  Structural conflicts of interest. Institutions may be required to investigate
          their own members, creating the classic problem expressed in quis custodiet
         ipsos custodes. The principle nemo judex in causa sua supports independent
        review where the home institution has an institutional interest in the
        outcome.
       4.  Existing normative foundation. The elucidation of Article 2 of Law No. 28 of
       1999 includes judges, prosecutors, investigators, and court clerks among
         public officials subject to wealth-reporting obligations. The missing element
            is  a  legal  consequence  for  objectively  unexplained  and  materially
         disproportionate wealth.

Elements of a Heightened Standard
       1. A lower materiality trigger than for ordinary subjects may be justified
        because the lawful income profile of many law-enforcement officers can be
          verified from public salary and employment records.
       2. LHKPN examination should be substantive and periodic rather than merely
          administrative, including a legally regulated lifestyle-audit component.
       3. The look-back period may encompass the period in office and a defined
         period after retirement or departure, subject to reasonable document-
         retention expectations.
       4.  Assets registered in the name of a spouse, child, or nominee may be examined
        where evidence indicates beneficial control, while retaining a rebuttable
        presumption and protection for bona fide third parties.
       5.  Ethical and employment consequences may operate in parallel under their
       own legal bases, while criminal liability remains subject to the separate
         criminal process and criminal standard of proof.
     Applications  involving law-enforcement  officers should not be  controlled
exclusively by the institution in which the officer serves. The proposed design places
application  authority  in  the Corruption  Eradication Commission (KPK) or an
independent asset-recovery  unit, subject to adjudication by a designated Anti-
Corruption Court panel. Where judges are concerned, external oversight by the Judicial
Commission may support accountability without displacing the court’s adjudicative
authority.
     Heightened scrutiny does not mean diminished rights. All safeguards applicable
at Stage 4 remain available to law-enforcement officers. The differentiation is based on
legally relevant differences in public authority, fiduciary responsibility, and structural
conflict risk rather than on an assumption of guilt. The framework must therefore
continue to comply with Article 28D(1) and Article 28I(2) of the 1945 Constitution.
    The proposed reform is a dedicated chapter on unexplained-wealth confiscation
within future asset-forfeiture legislation, accompanied by a specific law-enforcement-
officer clause and coordinated amendments to related statutes.
       1. A tiered balanced reversed-burden model. The balance-of-probabilities idea
            is developed from its traditional criminal-procedure context into a four-stage
          in rem procedure, with a different evidentiary standard at each stage.
       2. A heightened standard for law-enforcement officers. Rather than treating
         law-enforcement officers in exactly the same way as all public officials, this
           article formulates enhanced scrutiny based on their greater coercive power
        and fiduciary duties.
       3. An  independent-examiner  principle. Nemo  judex  in  causa  sua   is
         operationalised as a procedural requirement rather than being left as an
          ethical aspiration.
       4. A reorientation of LHKPN. Wealth declarations are treated not merely as an
         administrative reporting duty but as one lawful source of objective triggering
         evidence.
       5.  Express  rights  safeguards,  including  State compensation  for wrongful
          confiscation, to answer due-process concerns raised in debates over non-
         conviction-based confiscation.15

Proposed Norms (Ius Constituendum)
    The  following  clauses  are academic proposals  for  future  asset-forfeiture
legislation and are not presented as enacted law.
Article A - Confiscation of Unexplained Wealth
       1.  Property whose value is materially disproportionate to the owner’s lawful
        income and whose lawful origin cannot be reasonably explained may be
         confiscated for the State by court order.
       2. An application may be filed only where the difference between the value of
         the property and cumulative lawful income exceeds a threshold prescribed by
          law.
       3. The applicant must establish reasonable grounds for suspecting the material
          disparity referred to in paragraph (1).
       4. Where the applicant has satisfied paragraph (3), the property holder must
          establish the lawful origin of the property on the balance of probabilities.
       5.  Information supplied by the property holder solely in the confiscation
         proceeding may not be used as evidence to prosecute that person criminally,
         except in proceedings concerning intentionally false statements.
Article B - Law-Enforcement Officers
       1. A lower statutory trigger and a longer defined look-back period may apply to
          investigators, public prosecutors, judges, and court clerks, taking account of
         the term of office and a specified period after office.
       2. An application against a law-enforcement  officer may not be  filed or
         controlled exclusively by the institution in which that officer serves.
       3.  Property registered in the name of a spouse, child, or another person but
        demonstrably controlled for the benefit of the law-enforcement officer may
        be treated as property of the officer unless the contrary is established.
Article C - Protection of Rights
       1. A bona fide third party may object to an application and submit evidence of
        independent lawful ownership.
       2. Where a confiscation order is annulled by a final and binding decision, the
          State must return the property and provide compensation in accordance with
          law.

      Institutional resistance is particularly important for the law-enforcement-officer
clause. A heightened evidentiary standard will be ineffective if the body responsible for
initiating the process has a structural interest in protecting  its own members.
Independence of the applicant and transparency of the judicial process are therefore
essential design features.
     Asset recovery also does not end when a court makes a confiscation order.
Property that is perishable, costly to maintain, or exposed to market fluctuation may
lose value if it is not professionally managed. Legislation should therefore regulate
inventory,  valuation,  storage, audited  early  sale  in appropriate  circumstances,
reporting, and transparent use of proceeds. A State that requires individuals to explain

the provenance of property should itself be able to account publicly for the management

    The relationship between provisional restraint and final confiscation must also be
defined. Temporary freezing or seizure may be necessary to prevent dissipation, but it
should be based on judicial authorisation or prompt judicial review, limited in time, and
subject to challenge. The law should permit reasonable living expenses, legal costs, and
the continuation of legitimate business activities where these do not dissipate the value
of the restrained property. These limitations preserve proportionality while keeping
asset-conservation measures effective.
    A clear look-back period is equally important. Without a temporal limit, a
property holder may be required to explain transactions so old that supporting
documents can no longer reasonably be expected to exist. For law-enforcement officers,
the period may be linked to the time in office and a defined period thereafter, but it
should remain compatible with reasonable record-retention expectations. Transitional
rules are also necessary so that a new in rem mechanism is not perceived as retroactive
criminal punishment.
    The objective trigger should combine quantitative and qualitative indicators. A
quantitative indicator may consist of a minimum disparity between lawful income and
total asset value. Qualitative indicators may include transaction patterns, nominee
structures, control inconsistent with the person’s economic profile, or verified financial
intelligence. A large asset is not necessarily unlawful, and a complex ownership
structure is not necessarily criminal. The relevant question is whether a combination of
verifiable facts creates reasonable grounds to require an explanation.
Normative Parameters to Prevent Arbitrary Confiscation
    The strength of an unexplained-wealth regime should not be measured by how
easily the State can confiscate property. Its legitimacy depends on its ability to reconcile
effective asset recovery with control over public power. Rules that are too weak leave
criminal proceeds beyond practical recovery, while rules that are too broad threaten
legal certainty and property rights. A staged, measurable, rebuttable, and judicially
supervised design is therefore the central condition for compatibility with the rule of
law.
    The law must also distinguish in rem consequences from criminal, ethical, and
administrative consequences. A finding that property remains unexplained should not
automatically amount to a criminal finding that the holder committed corruption. If the
evidence creates reasonable suspicion of a criminal offence, criminal proceedings must
follow the applicable criminal procedure and evidentiary standards. This separation
prevents asset-recovery law from becoming a shortcut around the higher standard of
criminal proof.
     For legislative drafting, open-ended expressions such as “unreasonable wealth”
should be accompanied by measurable indicators. The statute should specify how
lawful income is calculated, how assets are valued, how appreciation is treated, how
inheritance and gifts are verified, how jointly owned property and debt are handled,
how business income  is assessed, and how foreign assets are treated. Defined
parameters assist courts in achieving consistent decisions and enable the public to
understand what evidence may reasonably be required to explain the origin of
property.

are often conflated: when the State may begin examining a wealth disparity; when the

CONCLUSION
    The unexplained wealth doctrine can be developed as an autonomous basis for
asset confiscation in Indonesia  if  it is constructed as a clearly regulated in rem
mechanism under judicial control and with meaningful rights of defence. A reversed
evidentiary burden becomes legitimate only after the State objectively demonstrates a
material disparity between property and lawful income and satisfies a defined prima
facie burden.
    The proposed fair formulation is a tiered balanced reversed-burden model: an
objective trigger; prima facie proof by the State; an opportunity for the property holder
to establish lawful origin on the balance of probabilities; and a judicial determination
directed only at the portion of property that remains unexplained. The structure seeks
to reconcile the public interest in recovering illicit assets with legal certainty, property
rights, and due process.
     Application to law-enforcement officers requires stronger verification because of
their coercive authority and the structural conflicts that may arise when institutions
examine their own members. That strengthening should take the form of verified
wealth scrutiny, independent initiation and examination, and transparent judicial
oversight, while preserving the same due-process guarantees and protections for bona
fide third parties.
