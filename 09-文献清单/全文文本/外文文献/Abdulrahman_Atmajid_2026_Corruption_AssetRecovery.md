I. INTRODUCTION
     Corruption is ordinarily described as a crime, and Indonesian policy has been
constructed  accordingly.  The  anti-corruption  legislation  defines  offences,
establishes an aggravated penalty structure and creates specialised courts and a
dedicated commission; the anti-money laundering framework adds predicate
offence liability and reporting obligations.1 Measured by convictions, the framework
performs. Measured by recovery of what was taken, it does not: the value returned
to the treasury has consistently represented a minority share  of  judicially
established state losses.
     This divergence is not accidental but structural. A conviction-centred model
attaches liability to persons, while proceeds are held by companies, nominees,
family members and foreign accounts. It requires proof beyond reasonable doubt of
individual culpability before any asset consequence follows, so that the death, flight
or incapacity of the accused terminates the claim to property along with the
prosecution. And  it places the entire burden of tracing on prosecutors whose
resources are calibrated to case counts rather than to forensic accounting across
jurisdictions.
     International  practice has developed a  response. The United Nations
Convention against Corruption requires states to enable confiscation, to provide for
mutual assistance in recovery, and invites consideration of confiscation without
criminal conviction in cases where the offender cannot be prosecuted by reason of
death, flight or absence.2 Indonesia ratified the Convention in 2006, and a bill on
asset forfeiture has been under discussion for well over a decade without
enactment.3

The ideal condition would combine punishment with restitution, treating the
two as complementary rather than sequential. The actual condition treats recovery
as an incident of conviction. The result is a policy that satisfies the demand for
retribution  while  leaving  the  material consequences  of  corruption  largely
undisturbed, which is precisely the configuration most corrosive of public trust:
visible prosecutions accompanied by visibly unrecovered wealth.
    The literature divides. Asset recovery scholarship, developed substantially
through the work associated with the Stolen Asset Recovery initiative, documents
the practical mechanics of tracing and the comparative performance of in rem
regimes.4 Criminal law scholarship has raised sustained objections to forfeiture
without conviction, concerning the presumption of innocence, the reversal of
evidentiary burdens and the protection of third parties. Islamic legal scholarship has
examined misappropriation of public property through the doctrines of ghulūl and
khiyāna, generally in an ethical register.
    The gap lies in the absence of an integrated treatment. Recovery scholarship
rarely engages the constitutional objections seriously enough to answer them;
criminal law scholarship rarely engages the empirical failure that motivates the
reform; and Islamic legal doctrine, which contains a developed law of restitution
operating independently of punishment, is almost never brought into the design
conversation. This article asks: where does conviction-centred recovery structurally
fail; what safeguards would make in rem forfeiture constitutionally defensible; and
what does Islamic legal doctrine contribute to the separation of restitution from
punishment?

II. RESEARCH METHODS
    The research is normative juridical, applying statutory, conceptual, case and
comparative approaches.5 The statutory approach examines the anti-corruption and
anti-money laundering legislation, the Criminal Procedure Code provisions on
seizure,  the  state  finance and  treasury  legislation, and  the Convention  as
incorporated. The conceptual approach reconstructs the distinction between in
personam and in rem proceedings, the standards of proof attaching to each, and the
Islamic doctrines governing unlawfully acquired property. The case approach
analyses  judicial reasoning on  state  loss and on  substitute payment. The
comparative approach considers Italy, Ireland and Australia as jurisdictions with
established civil forfeiture regimes of differing designs.

III. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

A. Three Structural Failure Points
    The first finding identifies where recovery fails. The initial failure point is the
mismatch between the subject of liability and the location of assets. Criminal liability
attaches to natural persons; proceeds are held by legal entities, relatives and
nominees who are not accused of anything. Unless the prosecution can establish that
the holder knew of the unlawful origin, the asset is beyond reach, and the standard
of proof required makes that demonstration difficult precisely where structuring
has been most sophisticated.
    The second failure point is evidentiary and resource-based. Tracing proceeds
through layered corporate  structures and  across borders  requires  forensic
accounting capacity, sustained international cooperation and time measured in
years. Prosecutorial resources are allocated to case completion, and a case is
complete on conviction. The institutional incentive therefore terminates at precisely
the moment when recovery work would need to intensify.
    The third failure point is procedural termination. Where the accused dies,
absconds or becomes permanently unfit, the criminal proceeding ends. Because the
asset consequence is an incident of that proceeding, the claim to the property ends
with it, notwithstanding that the property's unlawful origin may be independently
demonstrable. This is the situation the Convention expressly contemplates in
inviting states to consider confiscation without conviction, and it is the clearest case
for reform because it involves no contested question of guilt at all.

B. In Rem Forfeiture and Its Due Process Objections
    The second finding concerns the alternative and its costs. Non-conviction
based forfeiture proceeds against the asset rather than the person, is characterised
as civil, and applies the balance of probabilities rather than the criminal standard.
Its attraction is that it detaches restitution from the fate of the accused. Its danger is
that the same features can convert it into punishment without trial.
     Three  objections  deserve  direct  answers  rather  than  dismissal. The
presumption of innocence objection is met by confining the proceeding to the lawful
origin of property and by prohibiting the use of forfeiture findings as evidence of
guilt in any criminal case. The burden of proof objection is met by requiring the state
to establish a prima facie case of unlawful origin before any evidential burden shifts,
rather than by imposing a general obligation on individuals to justify their wealth.

The third party objection is met by mandatory notice, a right of intervention and
protection for purchasers in good faith without notice.
     Comparative experience indicates that these safeguards are workable. The
Irish regime, operated through a specialised bureau with judicial authorisation at
each stage, has functioned for decades without the systemic abuses predicted at its
introduction, largely because judicial control is continuous rather than terminal.6
The Italian preventive measures regime, by contrast, illustrates the opposite lesson:
where the link between the asset and specified unlawful conduct is loosened too far,
the regime attracts sustained human rights challenge. The design margin  is
therefore narrow but real.

C. Islamic Doctrine: Restitution Independent of Punishment
    The third finding is doctrinal and, for the Indonesian debate, decisive. Islamic
law treats the misappropriation of public property as a distinct wrong, ghulūl,
understood as the covert taking of property from a common fund by one entrusted
with  it, and the Qurʾānic treatment emphasises that what is misappropriated
remains attached to the taker as a liability.7 The prophetic tradition concerning the
collector who claimed part of the revenue as a gift establishes that property received
by reason of office belongs to the public fund rather than to the officeholder.8
    Two structural features follow. First, the obligation to return is a civil liability
attaching to the property, distinct from any discretionary penalty imposed for the
wrong; restitution and punishment operate on separate tracks, and the extinction of
the second does not extinguish the first. Second, classical practice developed a public
inspection of unexplained wealth among officeholders, associated with the second
caliph's requirement that officials account for property acquired in office, which is
functionally an accounting obligation rather than a criminal charge.9
    Read as policy design rather than as moral exhortation, this tradition supports
precisely the separation that in rem forfeiture requires: the state's claim to
misappropriated public property is not derivative of its power to punish, and the
death of the wrongdoer transfers the obligation of restitution to the estate rather
than extinguishing it. Indonesian reform can therefore be defended on doctrinal
grounds internal to the tradition, which matters because forfeiture legislation is
frequently resisted as an alien importation inconsistent with established criminal
law principle.

    The comparison suggests that the durability of a forfeiture regime correlates
with the continuity of judicial control rather than with the strictness of its standard
of  proof. Regimes  in which a court authorises each coercive step survive
constitutional challenge; regimes relying on administrative determination with
subsequent review do not. For Indonesia this indicates that the pending bill should
be evaluated primarily by the density of judicial authorisation it requires, not by the
breadth of assets it reaches.

E. Criteria for a Defensible Recovery Policy
     Five criteria follow. First, statutory separation of restitution from punishment,
establishing an in rem action that may proceed where prosecution is impossible by
reason of death, flight or incapacity. Second, judicial authorisation at each coercive
stage, from freezing to final forfeiture, with reasoned decisions subject to appeal.
Third, a defined evidential sequence in which the state must first establish a prima
facie case of unlawful origin, after which the holder may be required to account for
the property's provenance. Fourth, third party protection through mandatory
notice, a right of intervention and the exclusion of bona fide purchasers without
notice. Fifth, transparent disposition, with recovered assets recorded in the state
budget and reported publicly, since a recovery regime whose proceeds are
untraceable reproduces the very opacity it exists to remedy.
     Applied together, these criteria answer the objections that have stalled the
reform without diluting its purpose. They also align with the doctrinal structure
identified above, in which the obligation to restore misappropriated public property
is a liability attaching to the property itself and surviving the person who took it.

IV. CONCLUSION
     Indonesian anti-corruption policy recovers little because it is designed to
punish rather than to restore. Liability attaches to persons while assets sit with
entities and relatives; tracing capacity is not resourced past conviction; and the
proceeding's termination through death or flight extinguishes the claim to property
whose unlawful origin may be independently demonstrable. These are structural
features of the model, not failures of effort.
     Non-conviction based forfeiture addresses each failure point, and its due
process objections are answerable by design rather than by assertion: confinement
of the proceeding to the origin of property, a state-borne prima facie burden,
continuous judicial authorisation, and protection of third parties. Islamic legal
doctrine supports the same separation, treating the restitution of misappropriated
public property as a liability attaching to the property and surviving the wrongdoer,
with the accounting of officeholders operating independently of punishment.
    The study recommends enacting an in rem forfeiture statute built on those five
criteria, together with transparent recording of recovered assets in the state budget.
Further research should examine the recovery ratio of Indonesian corruption
judgments over a defined period, disaggregating substitute payments from actual
asset recovery, since that disaggregation would establish the precise magnitude of
the gap this reform is intended to close.
